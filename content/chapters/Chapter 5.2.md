When in Rome, do as the Romans do, they say. So when in a temple, let’s do some volunteering for the good of the people. There is also the small fact that I’m completely in debt to them for my treatment, but such trifling matters do not sway my charitable heart. Thus, acting on the express recommendation of Myra, I made my way to the opposite wing of the building.

As expected of a temple, this was another giant prayer hall. They call it the Blessings Hall. _Or was it the Hall of Blessings? Does it matter, when all these people do is pray, pray and pray._ Instead of a podium and rows of seats like the central Prayer Hall, it was divided into seven smaller sections. Each housed a shrine to one of the elemental gods, who as far as I could tell were Celestia’s middle management, with an appointed priest performing rituals for the visitors.

I looked around for someone in charge and approached the priest in the gaudiest robe. From my limited understanding of the temple hierarchy, the costlier the clothes, the higher the priest sits on the food chain. Myra always wears a plain white robe with little decoration. Eleanor, befitting her position as head of the institution, always wears a very classy green one. This man had neither class nor taste, but it was probably wise to show him some deference, since his robe screamed, “Look at me, I’m an important person!”

“Hello, sir. I’ve been told you’re short on manpower here. Is there anything I can do to help?”

“Ehm, who are you?” he asked, narrowing his eyes.

“I’m Jay. I was hospitalized here a week ago, but I’m better now. I’m staying in the rear wing thanks to the temple’s grace, so I thought I’d offer some help in return.”

“Must be another rat that Eleanor picked up,” he mumbled to himself. _Hey! If you’re going to be rude, at least be subtle about it._

“Can you do any math?” he finally asked.

“Yes, I’m very good at it.”

“Okay, come with me.” He led me to what looked like a registration counter. “Sit here and help out at this counter.”

“Just letting you know beforehand, I can’t open the blue status screen that you all have.” I disclosed my glaring weakness upfront, to be safe.

“Can’t open the status screen? Ah, I’ve heard about that. You must be the kid forsaken by the Goddess.” He nodded knowingly.

_Wait, when was I given such a crappy title?!_

“It’s fine. We have a tool that even the likes of you should be able to use.”

He pointed at a device that looked exactly like the call bell on a hotel reception desk.

“People who want blessings from the gods come here first to collect the respective tokens. First, tap this bell and ask them to select the blessings they want.” He tapped the bell, and a screen popped up in front of us. It was exactly like a shop menu in a game, listing items (blessings, in this case) alongside their prices.

“Once they confirm their selection, the screen shows the total they’ve agreed to donate. Collect that amount, hand over the requested tokens, and give change if needed. Then tap the bell again to end the donation and move on to the next person in the queue.”

I nodded along, which seemed to irk him. He narrowed his eyes again and pointed a finger at me.

“Always remember to tap the bell after accepting the donation. Only then does it record the amount. We tally the entire collection at the end of the day, so if there’s a discrepancy, we’ll know immediately who is responsible. Don’t think you can fool us and pocket some change.”

“The thought never crossed my mind,” I replied stiffly. What an unwarranted accusation.

He gave me a disapproving sneer. “Even if you forget to tap by mistake, it will be counted against your name, and you will be punished accordingly. Be very careful handling the money.”

“Yes, sir.”

“Now show me you can do this adequately,” he said, pointing at the seat.

I took my spot and called the first person in the queue forward. I tapped the bell, let them select their blessings, accepted the cash, and handed over the change and tokens. Smooth, if I say so myself. I’d manned far harder stalls during college fests, so this was a breeze.

After watching me for a bit, the priest seemed to finally trust me, because he left without another word. What had started as a leisurely day had turned into a grunt desk job. Secretly, though, I was happy to see a game-like screen again. It felt like being back home, playing games from my couch. A safe space where I could chill without restraint, something I sorely lacked right now.

--------
Working at the desk itself was drab, but I learned a lot of trivia about the gods. While Celestia was the one true goddess, she didn’t tend to every little task by herself. She bequeathed some of her powers to seven subordinate gods who managed the day-to-day activities of this world on her behalf.

Obtaining blessings from these gods would apparently help you at your job. For example, a lot of knights were seeking the blessings of Ignareth, the god of fire, as it made you physically stronger. Merchants seemed to prefer Aquaresia, the goddess of water. If you were granted her blessings, then apparently money would move through your business like the streams of a great river. As a business student myself, I would have sent my prayers to her before my exams. Or maybe it should be to Fulgarian, the god of lightning, as education comes under his purview.

Overwhelmed already? I didn’t even touch upon the other elements like earth, wind, light, and darkness. It’s a mess regarding who governs what. Whenever someone tried to ask me which god they should pray to, I deftly avoided the question by telling them to seek answers from a priest. Poor folks. They probably ended up asking me because there weren’t many priests around in the first place. On top of that, they were being charged a good amount for the blessings themselves. Oh yes, not charged, but recommended to donate. Because the temple wasn’t an evil capitalistic megacorp that promises grand things and extorts a premium from you for services that have questionable benefits to your life. Yes, I cannot see any similarities between them and this benevolent institution.

A bell rang through the temple, signaling the closing of the gates, which sent the last few visitors scurrying away, lest they be locked inside with us. I was stretching out my stiff muscles, sore from sitting all day, when the priest from before came by again.

“How many people have come by today?” he asked.

“I was busy non-stop the whole day, so I’d say the turnout was good. Though I can’t say how it compares to a typical day, since this is my first time here,” I replied, and handed over a bag containing the day’s collection. His face broke into a slight smirk as he felt the pleasant weight of the coins in his hands. It lasted only a moment before he went right back to scowling at me.

“What are you looking at my face for? Hand over the bell as well.”

“Yes, sir.”

He snatched the bell from my hands and walked away, grumbling about how useless everyone around him was. Jeez, take a chill pill, dude. You don’t need to have a stick up your ass after I put in a good day’s work for free. It seems I’ll need to start charging them for my services if I want to earn some respect. Unfortunately for me, my bad luck shows no sign of ending.
